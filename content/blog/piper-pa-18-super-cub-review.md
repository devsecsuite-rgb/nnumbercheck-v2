Few aircraft in the history of general aviation evoke the spirit of adventure like the Piper PA-18 Super Cub. Introduced in 1949 as an evolution of the iconic J-3 Cub and PA-11 Cub Special, the PA-18 quickly established itself as the undisputed benchmark for Short Takeoff and Landing (STOL) utility aircraft. Decades after the final factory airframe rolled off the assembly line in Lock Haven, Pennsylvania, the Super Cub remains the gold standard for bush pilots, backcountry enthusiasts, and commercial operators worldwide.

## 1. Flight Characteristics & Operational Strengths

The primary reason buyers seek out a PA-18 Super Cub is uncompromised low-speed handling and off-airport capability. Piper took the gentle handling of the original J-3 and added electrical systems, flaps, and significantly more horsepower, transforming a docile trainer into a backcountry powerhouse.

### STOL Performance and Handling
* **Stall Speed:** With full flaps, a standard Super Cub stalls at a remarkably low 38 to 43 mph. The stall is forgiving, usually presenting a gentle nose drop with prompt recovery upon relaxing backpressure.
* **Takeoff & Landing Roll:** In the hands of a proficient tailwheel pilot, a 150-hp or 160-hp PA-18 can get airborne in under 300 feet and land in even less space.
* **Control Response:** High-aspect-ratio wings paired with balanced elevator and rudder controls give the Super Cub incredible control authority at slow airspeeds. However, heavy control forces—especially in roll due to the long span ailerons—mean flying a Cub requires active, coordinated stick-and-rudder technique.

### Cruise and Payload Reality
The Super Cub was not built for cross-country speed. Expect a honest cruise speed between **95 and 115 knots**, depending on wheel size, engine option, and aerodynamic drag from external modifications. 

Useful loads typically range from **800 to over 1,000 lbs**, depending on gross weight STCs (such as 1,750 lb, 2,000 lb, or 2,200 lb gross weight upgrades). Fuel capacity standardly sits at 36 gallons (two 18-gallon wing tanks), though many aircraft feature 24-gallon or 30-gallon auxiliary tank modifications for extended backcountry endurance.

## 2. Model Evolution & Engine Configurations

Over its 40+ year production run, Piper produced several variants of the PA-18. When shopping the pre-owned market, understanding which variant you are evaluating is critical to setting market value:

1. **PA-18-95 Cub (1949–1954):** Powered by the Continental C-90 engine without flaps. Light and nimble, but lacks the raw STOL power of later models.
2. **PA-18-105 / 125 / 135 (1950–1954):** Introduced Lycoming O-230 and O-290 engines, adding wing flaps and balanced elevators.
3. **PA-18-150 / 160 Super Cub (1954–1983, 1988–1994):** The definitive Super Cub. Powered by the legendary Lycoming O-320 (150 hp or 160 hp), featuring dual wing tanks, flaps, and standard 1,750 lb gross weight.
4. **PA-18A:** The agricultural variant built for crop dusting and spraying. Features a flat deck, modified fuselage structure, and dust hopper installation capabilities. Many have since been converted back to standard utility seating.

### Common Aftermarket Modifications
It is rare to find an all-original PA-18. Most aircraft in active service feature high-value Supplemental Type Certificates (STCs):
* **180-HP Lycoming O-360 Upgrades:** Ideal for high-density altitude and heavy load operations.
* **Alaskan Bushwheels & Heavy-Duty Landing Gear:** 29-inch to 35-inch tundra tires paired with 3-inch extended heavy-duty landing gear legs.
* **Vortex Generators (VGs):** Decreases stall speed by several knots and improves low-speed lateral control.
* **Extended Wings and Droop Tips:** Increases wing area for improved lift.

## 4. Key Pre-Purchase Inspection Points & Airworthiness Directives

When inspecting a prospective Piper PA-18 airframe, specialized taildragger mechanics focus heavily on structural integrity and fabric age.

### Airframe Fabric & Frame Structure
* **Tubular Steel Frame Corrosion:** The PA-18 fuselage is constructed of welded 4130 chromoly steel tubing wrapped in fabric. Moisture can settle inside the lower longerons and tail section, leading to severe internal rusting. A thorough inspection requires removing belly panels and performing ultrasonic thickness testing or punch tests on suspect tubes.
* **Fabric Condition:** Whether covered in Ceconite, Poly-Fiber, or Oratex, check the age of the fabric, punch test readings, and signs of UV degradation or ring-cracking in the finish. Replacing fabric is a major labor effort costing tens of thousands of dollars.

### Critical Airworthiness Directives (ADs)
* **Wing Lift Struts (AD 99-01-05):** Mandates regular inspections or replacement of wing lift struts to prevent internal corrosion failure. Upgrading to heavy-duty, sealed lift struts eliminates the recurring inspection requirement.
* **Tail Rotor & Stabilizer Attachment:** Inspect the jackscrew mechanism for trim control and look for cracking around the horizontal stabilizer attachment points.
* **Bungee and Gear Strut Attach Points:** The rubber shock bungees require periodic replacement, and the landing gear attach fittings on the lower fuselage structure must be inspected for stress fractures.

## 5. Why Checking an Aircraft's N-Number History is Critical

Given that a significant portion of the surviving Super Cub fleet has engaged in off-airport flying, forestry patrol, or towing operations, **verifying an aircraft's complete paper trail is the single most important step in the buying process.**

It is not uncommon for a PA-18 to undergo a complete fuselage frame replacement or major wing rebuild following a landing incident. If performed correctly by a certified repair station or A&P/IA with proper Form 337 filings, major repairs are perfectly safe and structurally sound. However, problems arise when:

* Damage history is omitted or undisclosed by seller representatives.
* Frame rebuilds were executed off the books without proper FAA field approvals or Form 337 entries.
* Airworthiness Directives (such as lift strut inspections) were signed off without completing the required physical work.
* An airframe was assembled from multiple donor parts, creating title discrepancies or registration conflicts.

By checking the registration history, historical NTSB records, and title status before committing earnest money or traveling for a pre-purchase inspection, you protect yourself against costly surprises.
