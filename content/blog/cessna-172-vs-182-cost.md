## 1. Initial Acquisition Costs: Skyhawk vs. Skylane

Acquisition cost is the first major financial hurdle. Because both airframes span generations of production (from the mid-1950s to brand-new factory models), market prices vary significantly based on avionics fitments, airframe hours, and engine times.

```
+------------------------+---------------------+---------------------+
| Vintage / Generation   | Cessna 172 (Skyhawk)| Cessna 182 (Skylane)|
+------------------------+---------------------+---------------------+
| Legacy (1960s - 1970s) | $65,000 - $110,000  | $95,000 - $155,000  |
| Late Model (1980s-90s) | $120,000 - $185,000 | $165,000 - $240,000 |
| Modern G1000 (2004+)   | $220,000 - $400,000 | $310,000 - $550,000 |
| Factory New (2023+)    | $475,000 - $530,000 | $650,000 - $720,000 |
+------------------------+---------------------+---------------------+
```

### The Purchase Price Premium
On average, a Cessna 182 commands a **30% to 50% price premium** over a Cessna 172 of comparable age, avionics suite, and airframe condition. 

* **Cessna 172 Value Drivers:** High demand from flight schools keeps 172 values remarkably stable. If you buy a mid-time Skyhawk, run it for 300 hours over three years, and maintain it well, depreciation is negligible.
* **Cessna 182 Value Drivers:** Private cross-country missions drive 182 sales. Models equipped with modern autopilot systems (like the Garmin GFC 500) and updated glass panels command top dollar due to high demand among instrument-rated private pilots.

## 2. Variable Operating Costs: Hourly Expense Realities

Variable costs fluctuate directly with the number of hours you fly each year. This is where the physical differences between the 4-cylinder Skyhawk and the 6-cylinder Skylane become starkly evident.

### Fuel Consumption (100LL)
* **Cessna 172 (Lycoming O-320 / IO-360):** Typically burns **7.5 to 9.5 gallons per hour (GPH)** at standard cruise settings (110–122 knots). At an average rate of $6.50/gallon for 100LL, your fuel cost is roughly **$48 to $62 per hour**.
* **Cessna 182 (Continental O-470 / Lycoming IO-540):** Burns **12.0 to 14.5 GPH** at cruise settings (135–150 knots). At $6.50/gallon, your fuel cost jumps to **$78 to $94 per hour**.

### Engine Overhaul Reserves
* **Cessna 172:** Standard Lycoming engines carry a 2,000-hour Time Between Overhauls (TBO). A top-tier overhaul runs approximately $30,000 to $36,000, creating an overhaul reserve obligation of **$15.00 to $18.00 per hour**.
* **Cessna 182:** The larger 6-cylinder Continental O-470 or Lycoming IO-540 carries a 1,500 to 2,000-hour TBO. Overhauling a 6-cylinder powerplant—plus servicing or replacing the constant-speed propeller and governor—costs between $42,000 and $52,000. Your hourly engine/prop reserve escalates to **$24.00 to $30.00 per hour**.

**Total Hourly Direct Operating Cost Summary:**
* **Cessna 172:** ~$70 to $90 / hour
* **Cessna 182:** ~$115 to $145 / hour

## 3. Fixed Costs: Hangar, Insurance, and Maintenance

Fixed costs accrue whether your aircraft flies 100 hours a year or sits idle in the hangar. 

### Insurance Differences
Insurance underwriting criteria treat the 172 and 182 quite differently:

* **Cessna 172:** Considered the standard for low-risk light single-engine aircraft. A low-time private pilot can expect to pay **$1,200 to $1,800 annually** for standard hull ($100k) and smooth liability coverage.
* **Cessna 182:** Classified as a high-performance aircraft (230+ horsepower). Insurers often require a high-performance endorsement, logged transitions hours, or 100+ total hours for low rates. Annual premiums for a low-time pilot typically range from **$2,200 to $4,200**, depending on hull value.

### Annual Inspections & Maintenance
While both airframes feature fixed tricycle landing gear, the Cessna 182 introduces mechanical complexity:
1. **Constant-Speed Propeller:** Requires periodic seal replacements, governor overhauls, and AD compliance.
2. **Cowl Flaps & Complex Fuel Systems:** Adds extra inspection points during annuals.
3. **6-Cylinder Engine Access:** Tighter engine cowlings require more shop labor hours.

A basic, clean annual inspection on a Cessna 172 averages **$1,500 to $2,500** (without major discrepancies). A basic annual on a Cessna 182 routinely costs **$2,500 to $4,000**.

### Critical Airworthiness Directives to Track
When evaluating specific candidate aircraft in the 172 and 182 market, historical records show several critical ADs that directly impact your maintenance budget:

* **AD 04-20774 & AD E8-1460:** Focuses on structural fatigue, wing spar attachments, and seat track locking mechanisms. Worn seat tracks pose a severe safety hazard during takeoff and landing if a seat slips backward.
* **AD 2010-28579:** Mandates inspection and replacement of specific fuel system valves and selector handles to prevent fuel starvation risks.
* **AD E8-3771 & AD 2011-11260:** Addresses critical engine and propeller blade stress cracking, hub inspections, and ignition/magneto impulse coupling failures. Failing to verify historical compliance with AD 2011-11260 can require an immediate, unbudgeted propeller or magneto replacement during your first annual inspection.

A single overlooked AD can cost between **$1,500 and $12,000** to rectify. That is why reviewing an airframe's historical logs against official database records is vital before making an offer.
